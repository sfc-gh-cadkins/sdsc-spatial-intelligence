Start with something everyone knows

You have probably filed an insurance claim after a storm.

Now think about the other side of that claim. Someone had to figure out how many properties were affected, how much exposure the company had, where to send adjusters - with a map, ideally by end of day.

That workflow - a location-dependent question, a specialist bottleneck, real time pressure - exists in almost every industry. Insurance is just where it hurts the most visibly.

The real bottleneck

It is not the data. It is who can use it.

The GIS talent gap

  • Geospatial analysis requires people who understand mapping, data science, and hazard modeling
  • Many insurance teams do not have that skill set in-house
  • Every spatial question routes through the same small team, with a backlog

What agents change

  • Spatial expertise gets encoded once, in a semantic view
  • An agent delivers it conversationally - no GIS tooling required to ask
  • The specialist's time shifts from running queries to building the layer everyone else uses

Source: A3Logics, "Geospatial Data for Better Risk Assessment and Underwriting." a3logics.com

What is changing in 2026

Four trends, all spatial at their core

01

Continuous underwriting

Monitoring risk factors in real time through the policy term, not just at annual renewal.

02

Regulatory pressure

Regulators now require climate risk to be acknowledged in underwriting strategy, with new compliance checks on AI-driven processes.

03

Parametric triggers

Policies that pay out automatically when a measurable spatial threshold is crossed - rainfall, wind speed, flood depth.

04

Remote claims verification

Cross-checking claims against objective geospatial evidence instead of sending an adjuster to every site.

Sources: Guidewire, "2026 P&C Insurance Trends"; Markel, "Top 10 insurance trends for 2026"; Spyrosoft, "How satellite data revolutionises risk assessment."

The point of this workshop

Insurance is the example. The pattern is universal.

Any workflow where someone needs a spatial answer but does not have GIS skills is the same problem.

  • Retail: Which stores are in the path of this storm, and should we pre-position inventory?
  • Logistics: Which routes avoid this closure, given our delivery windows?
  • Real estate: Which properties in our portfolio just had their flood risk reclassified?
  • Government: Where should we open shelters, relative to population density and road access?
  • Energy: Which substations are in the wildfire perimeter, and how many customers do they serve?
