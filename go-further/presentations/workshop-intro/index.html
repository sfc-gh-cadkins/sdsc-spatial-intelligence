Location is a type, not just two number columns. Snowflake has two: one respects that the Earth is round, the other lays it flat. We support both, plus all the common projections, and you can store shapes as well-known text, well-known binary or GeoJSON. None of it's proprietary. It's the same stuff you'd expect anywhere else. And yes, every spatial project still starts with: what projection is this data in now?

GEOGRAPHY treats the Earth as a sphere, the WGS84 coordinates your phone's GPS uses. Distances and areas come back in metres, measured along the Earth's surface. That's what we use today.

GEOMETRY is a flat, planar map. Its units come from the coordinate system you pick, like feet in a state plane. Use it when your data's already projected or you need planar math.

Step 1 query, before you run it

Which Manhattan ZIP codes filed the most Sandy claims? Real Sandy claims by Manhattan ZIP code, against real ZIP boundaries. I'm not showing you anything you haven't done before, but most of this room has never done it on Snowflake.

Here's what it does. Count Sandy claims per ZIP. Turn each ZIP boundary into a shape. Then for every ZIP: how big is it, how far is its middle from the southern tip of Manhattan, and is it within 3 km of the tip?

Point at the functions in the Step 1 query

Three functions:

  • ST_AREA and ST_CENTROID: how big, and where's the middle?
  • ST_DISTANCE: how far, in metres?
  • ST_DWITHIN, distance within: is it within a threshold?

No special apparatus. Just typical ST functions and a cross join, all natural SQL. Most people export a CSV, put it in their GIS tool, convert it, and re-import it. Here you're just done in Snowflake.

The distance you pick decides the answer you get. That's the most important habit in spatial analysis: say where you looked.

Caveat

These are whole-county totals for counties near Manhattan, not claims inside a circle. FEMA's coordinates aren't precise enough for that.

Scroll to Step 3 · Flood zones (before running)

So far we've asked where the claims were. Now: did FEMA see them coming? Every flood policy is priced by a FEMA flood zone. The high-risk zones, A and V, are what FEMA calls the Special Flood Hazard Area. If the flood maps were right, almost every claim should come from inside those zones. Let's check.

Walk the Step 3 SQL

Nothing spatial in this one. It's a COUNT_IF on the zone each policy was rated in, grouped by ZIP. Sometimes the most important spatial question is just a filter.

Run Step 3: results

Look at the Seaport and Tribeca: 8 to 10 percent outside the zone. That's the waterfront, and the map got it right. Now look at the East Village and Chelsea: about a third of their claims came from properties FEMA said were not high risk. And East Harlem, 12 kilometres from the tip, is half.

Across Manhattan, more than one in four Sandy claims came from outside the high-risk zone. The flood map said lower risk. The water disagreed.

The business point

If you're underwriting off the zone alone, those are the policies you priced as safe. That's the 70% we talked about at the start: underwriting without real geography. The zone is a line on a map; the loss doesn't always respect the line.

Caveat

One caveat: this is the zone at the time of the loss. FEMA has redrawn New York's maps since Sandy, partly because of exactly this.
